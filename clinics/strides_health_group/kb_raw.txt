Sports physiotherapy in Narre Warren

Strides Performance supports swimmers and young athletes through injury, rehabilitation and return to sport. Helping young athletes stay healthy enough to keep doing what they love. Selected appointments are also available in Nunawading.

Sport should build confidence, not take it away.

Most young athletes don’t come to physiotherapy because they want physiotherapy.

They come because something has interrupted the sport they love.

Aches, pains and injuries are part of sport. Understanding why they happen and how to respond can make all the difference.

Who We Work With

Whether you’re an athlete or a parent, it’s completely normal to have questions. Our role is to help you understand what’s happening, explain your options clearly and develop a plan that supports both your health and your long-term development.

We often help athletes who are…

Recovering from injury.

Returning to training or competition.

Experiencing growth-related pain.

Preparing for an important season.

Looking to reduce their risk of injury.

Wanting to improve strength, movement and resilience.

Trying to understand why injuries keep returning.

We also support athletes with a wide range of sporting conditions, including:

Tendon injuries

Growth-related conditions

Joint and muscle injuries

Concussion rehabilitation

Running injuries

Swimming-related shoulder pain

Load management

Return-to-sport planning

Performance support

Growing athletes aren’t just smaller adults. Growth, maturation, school demands, training and recovery can all influence how a young athlete feels and performs. Pain doesn’t always mean they need to stop completely, but pushing through isn’t always the right response either.

We look at the whole picture—their symptoms, sport, training load, stage of development and goals—to help them understand what may be contributing and decide on a sensible next step. Advice is tailored to the athlete and what they want to return to.

Every athlete is different, and every plan should be too.

We begin by understanding:

Your goals.

Your sport.

Your training load.

Your stage of growth and development.

Your recovery, school and life demands.

What matters most to you.

Treatment is guided by your goals and may include:

Movement assessment

Great athlete care rarely happens in isolation.

With your permission, we’re happy to work alongside parents, coaches, strength and conditioning staff, teachers, sports physicians and other health professionals so everyone is working towards the same goal.

Consistent communication often leads to better decisions, greater confidence and better outcomes for the athlete.

I’ve always believed physiotherapy for young athletes is about much more than treating injuries.

It’s about helping athletes understand their bodies, make good decisions, build resilience and continue enjoying the sport they love for years to come.

Whether you’re dealing with a recent injury, recurring pain, growth-related concerns or simply want to stay healthy throughout the season, we’re here when you’re ready.

Toe walking is when a child walks on the balls of their feet with their heels lifted. It can be part of early walking exploration, but a frequent or ongoing pattern is worth looking at in context. A physiotherapy assessment can check how your child moves, whether their heels can come down comfortably and whether another health professional should be involved.

What is toe walking?

Children learning to walk may sometimes rise onto their toes as they practise balance and explore movement. Many settle into a heel-to-toe pattern during the early years. If toe walking continues, it does not automatically mean there is a serious problem. The pattern, the child’s age and development, and any effect on comfort or everyday activities all matter.

When toe walking continues without an identifiable medical or developmental explanation, clinicians may use the term idiopathic toe walking. This is a diagnosis made after assessment has considered other possible explanations. Toe walking can also occur alongside a range of neurological, muscular, orthopaedic or developmental differences. A child’s walking pattern alone cannot identify the cause.

When should I seek advice?

Consider arranging an assessment if toe walking is frequent or ongoing, continues past age two, or has lasted more than six months after your child began walking independently. It is also reasonable to ask for advice whenever you are concerned about your child’s movement or participation.

Please seek medical advice sooner if toe walking is mainly on one side, has started after your child had been walking with their heels down, or is accompanied by pain, a limp, frequent falls, weakness, reduced ankle movement, or difficulty standing with the heels down. These features do not diagnose a particular condition, but they may mean that a medical review is needed.

Contact your GP or seek prompt medical advice if the change is sudden, worsening, painful, or occurs with weakness or a limp. If your child is acutely unwell or suddenly unable to walk or bear weight, seek urgent medical care.

What happens in an assessment?

A paediatric physiotherapist will ask about when the pattern began, how often it happens, whether it affects one or both sides, and what your child can do in play and daily activities. If it is helpful, a short video of your child walking at home can show what the pattern looks like in a familiar setting.

The assessment may include observing walking, running and balance; checking whether your child can stand with their heels down or walk on their heels; and looking at ankle movement, calf muscle length and size, strength, coordination and the spine. The physiotherapist will also consider your child’s overall development, medical history and your family’s priorities. If findings suggest another cause, they can recommend review by your GP, paediatrician or an appropriate specialist.

How can physiotherapy help?

Support is tailored to the child rather than to the toe-walking pattern alone. Some children may be monitored over time, while others may benefit from physiotherapy or medical input. If appropriate, physiotherapy may work on comfortable ankle movement, strength, balance, coordination and practising useful walking skills through activities the child enjoys. Strategies should be based on the assessment and linked to goals that matter to the child and family.

Depending on the findings and goals, the care team may discuss other options such as orthoses or serial casting. These need individual clinical consideration. More intensive treatment is not necessary for every child, and families should have a clear discussion of expected benefits, burdens and alternatives before making decisions.

What does the evidence say?

The evidence for treating idiopathic toe walking is limited. A Cochrane review found only four small studies with 104 participants, and the results were too uncertain to show whether adding botulinum toxin injections to serial casting improves outcomes compared with casting alone. This means treatment choices should be made carefully, based on assessment findings, the child’s function and comfort, and the family’s goals.

In one observational study, 79% of children who had toe walked at age five had stopped by age ten without treatment. This finding is reassuring, but it does not predict what will happen for an individual child or remove the need to assess concerning features.

A few helpful things to note

When did the toe walking begin, and was it present from the first steps?

How often does it happen, and is it on one side or both?

Can your child stand with their heels down, even briefly?

Have you noticed pain, falls, fatigue, tightness or changes in walking?

Does it affect play, running, shoes, stairs or other activities your child enjoys?

You do not need to correct every step or make your child practise stretches before an assessment. Keep play comfortable and let your child move in ways that feel safe. If you are unsure, start with your GP or a paediatric physiotherapist.

If you would like to talk through your concerns, contact Little Strides Physiotherapy to arrange an assessment. We can help you understand your child’s movement and agree on practical next steps.

A practical guide for swimmers, families and coaches

Knee pain can interrupt swimming, especially when breaststroke kicking is uncomfortable. “Swimmer’s knee” is a broad, informal label, not a diagnosis. Whether you swim for competition, recreation or fitness, the pattern of pain, training history and your wider health all matter. Early communication with your coach or a trusted support person can help you make sensible training changes while you work out what is contributing.

What people mean by swimmer’s knee

People often use “swimmer’s knee” to describe knee pain that appears during or after swimming. “Breaststroker’s knee” is a more specific descriptive term for pain associated with the breaststroke kick, often felt on the inner side of the knee. It still does not identify one particular injured structure. Older swimmer studies found that knee symptoms were reported more often in breaststroke swimmers, but knee pain can occur with other strokes too. The evidence is limited and largely based on older competitive-swimmer cohorts, so it cannot tell us how common the problem is among swimmers of all ages and levels today. [1,2]

A sore knee is not automatically caused by breaststroke. Front-of-knee pain can also show up with running, jumping, stairs or dryland training. In younger swimmers, growth-related conditions can cause pain around the bony attachment below the kneecap. A sudden injury or another health condition can also cause knee pain. An individual assessment helps distinguish these possibilities; the location of pain alone is not enough to diagnose it. [3,4]

What to notice

These observations can help you explain the pattern to a coach, family member or clinician. There is no need to test the knee or push into pain.

Where is the pain: inner side, front of the knee, below the kneecap or somewhere else?

Which activity brings it on: breaststroke kick, another stroke, turns, starts, running, jumping or stairs?

When does it begin, how does it change during the session, and is it still sore later or the next morning?

Has anything changed recently in breaststroke distance, kick sets, training frequency, dryland work, fins, competition or recovery?

Is walking, work, school activity, sleep or normal knee movement affected? Is there swelling, limping, locking or a feeling that the knee gives way?

What to do when pain starts

Tell someone you trust and, if you train with a coach, let them know. If you are a young swimmer, tell a parent or another trusted adult too. Early, calm adjustments are usually easier than waiting until pain affects your movement or makes you miss sessions.

Temporarily reduce or change the activity that clearly provokes pain. This may include breaststroke kicking, hard kick sets or a dryland drill. Do not force yourself to “kick through it”.

If other strokes or pull-buoy swimming feel comfortable, you can consider keeping those parts of training and discussing the plan with your coach if you have one. Younger swimmers should involve a parent or trusted adult. Stop or adjust if pain increases or changes your movement.

Review the recent total load across pool training, dryland, other sports and competitions, as well as rest and recovery. There is no single workload number or percentage that fits every swimmer.

Arrange an assessment if pain keeps returning, is worsening, limits training or everyday activity, causes a limp, or is not settling with sensible changes.

Training changes and a gradual return

A short-term change in training is not the same as giving up swimming. The aim is to find a level of activity you can do comfortably while an appropriate plan is made. A physiotherapist or doctor can assess the knee, consider your swimming and other activities, and guide rehabilitation if needed.

When symptoms are settling, build swimming back in steps. Start with the strokes and distances that feel comfortable, then add breaststroke kick or harder work gradually, ideally in discussion with your coach. Check how the knee feels during the session, later that day and the next morning. If pain starts to climb, affects technique, or lingers afterwards, reduce the most recent increase and get advice. Do not use a race date or a universal “10% rule” to decide readiness.

When to seek urgent medical care

Get prompt medical advice for a new, substantially swollen knee, a knee that locks or cannot straighten, a marked limp, or pain after a significant injury. Seek urgent care if you cannot bear weight, or have a hot, red, swollen knee with fever or feel unwell. [5]

How Strides Performance can help

A sports physiotherapy assessment can look at your pain pattern, knee movement and strength, training history, other activities and goals. For younger swimmers, growth and development can also be relevant. We can work with you and, where appropriate, your family and coach on practical training changes and a progressive return plan.

Returning to sport takes more than waiting for pain to settle

An injury can interrupt training, competition and the routines that matter to an athlete. Returning safely is usually a process rather than a single clearance day. It involves rebuilding the physical capacity, sport-specific skills and confidence needed to manage the demands of training and competition.

The right plan depends on the injury, the athlete, the sport and the level of participation they want to return to. Progress is guided by clinical assessment and the athlete’s response to loading, not by time alone. A date or test result cannot guarantee that reinjury will not occur.

The return-to-sport continuum

1. Return to participation

The athlete resumes selected or modified activity while rehabilitation continues. This may mean reduced training time, lower intensity, fewer drills, avoiding particular movements or taking part in non-contact components. The goal is to reintroduce sport in a manageable way.

2. Return to sport

The athlete progresses towards unrestricted training and competition. They build tolerance for the movements, speed, intensity, decision-making, contact and repeated efforts required by their sport. Training exposure is increased in planned steps and reviewed against symptoms and recovery.

3. Return to performance

The athlete works towards their previous level of performance, or a new agreed goal. Being back in competition does not necessarily mean strength, conditioning, confidence or performance have fully returned. Continued training and monitoring may be needed after the first games or events.

These stages can overlap, and progress is not always linear. An athlete may need to hold at a stage, modify the plan or step back temporarily if their response indicates that the current load is too much.

What guides progression?

There is no single test or universal threshold that suits every injury and sport. Readiness is considered across several areas:

Symptoms and recovery: how the injury responds during activity, afterwards and the following day.

Physical capacity: relevant strength, mobility, balance, power, endurance and repeated-effort capacity.

Sport-specific demands: the ability to perform the required skills, speeds, changes of direction, contact or workload safely and consistently.

Confidence and readiness: the athlete’s understanding, confidence and concerns about returning.

Context: training schedule, competition demands, other health factors, growth and maturation for young athletes, school or work demands, and available recovery.

A practical progression

Clarify the goal. Identify the sport, level, position or event and the demands the athlete needs to tolerate.

Rebuild general capacity. Restore the relevant movement, strength and fitness foundations through rehabilitation.

Reintroduce sport tasks. Start with controlled drills and planned modifications, then build complexity and intensity.

Build training exposure. Progress duration, frequency, intensity and competition-specific demands in planned steps. Avoid making large, abrupt increases where possible.

Review the response. Check symptoms, function, confidence and recovery before progressing. If symptoms flare or function drops, adjust the load and seek advice from the treating clinician.

For parents, coaches and support staff

The athlete’s treating health professional can help set clear, practical restrictions and milestones. With the athlete’s consent and, for young athletes, appropriate parent or guardian involvement, coaches can help adapt training and report how the athlete manages sport-specific tasks. Avoid pressuring an athlete to return for a particular game, selection date or event.

For adolescents, the plan should account for growth and maturation, school and family demands, other sports, training volume and recovery. Young athletes may need a coordinated plan between the athlete, family, clinician and coach.

When to pause and review

Stop or modify the session and contact the treating clinician if pain or swelling is increasing, movement or strength is worsening, the injury feels unstable, or symptoms remain noticeably worse after training or into the next day. Seek urgent medical care for severe or rapidly worsening symptoms, or if advised by a health professional.

Concussion needs its own return pathway

A suspected concussion requires immediate removal from play and medical assessment. Return after concussion follows a separate graded process and applicable Australian and sport-specific guidance. Do not use this general injury progression as concussion clearance.

Strides Performance connects injury rehabilitation with the demands of the athlete’s sport. Assessment may include relevant strength, movement and sport-specific measures, including VALD ForceDecks or Dynamo+ where they help answer a clinical question. Results are interpreted alongside symptoms, training history and clinical judgement; no single score determines readiness.

The aim is to help the athlete understand where they are, what they need to build and how to progress towards meaningful participation and performance. Book a sports physiotherapy appointment or contact Strides Health Group to discuss support.

A clearer picture of health, training and performance

Athlete screening is a structured conversation and assessment that helps guide the next step. It is not a pass/fail test, and no single test can reliably predict who will be injured.

Athlete screening can help an athlete, family, coach and health professional understand what is going well, what may need attention and what would make training safer or more productive. The right assessment depends on the athlete’s age, sport, training demands, health history and goals. It should end with practical priorities—not a label or a risk score on its own.

What can screening help with?

A useful screen can establish a starting point, identify symptoms that need clinical review, highlight relevant strengths or capacity gaps, and connect findings to a practical plan. It may be helpful before a new training phase, when an athlete is returning after injury or illness, when training demands change, or when performance, recovery or confidence has shifted.

For young athletes, screening also needs to account for development. Growth and maturation can change coordination, strength, tolerance to training and recovery needs. These changes vary between individuals and over time, so an assessment should be interpreted in context and repeated when the question or circumstances warrant it—not used to rank athletes by maturity.

What might an athlete screening include?

Screening is not one fixed battery. A clinician may select some or all of the following, depending on the reason for the assessment:

Health and participation history:
Current symptoms, relevant medical history, medication, previous injuries, concussion history where relevant, and any concerns that may affect safe participation. For young people, age-appropriate screening tools and parent or guardian input may be appropriate.

Injury and training history:
The type and timing of previous injuries, current pain or limitation, recent changes in training and competition, and how the athlete is responding to their overall load and recovery.

Development and wellbeing:
For developing athletes, growth and maturation context, sleep, energy, stress and recovery can help explain changes in readiness or performance. Sensitive topics such as eating, menstrual health or low energy availability should be approached respectfully, with privacy and an appropriate referral pathway.

Sport-specific physical capacity:
Where it answers a clear question, assessment may include movement, range of motion, strength, balance, power, speed, or a task that reflects the sport. Where appropriate, VALD ForceDecks and Dynamo+ can be used to assess selected aspects of jump performance, force production and strength, providing a baseline to monitor change. The test should match the athlete’s needs and be interpreted alongside their history and symptoms.

Goals and environment:
The athlete’s own priorities, competition calendar, access to strength training, coaching context and barriers to recovery can all shape what is realistic and useful.

What happens after the assessment?

The findings should be explained in plain language and linked to decisions. Depending on the results, the next step may be a strength or conditioning focus, an injury-prevention programme, a change to training exposure, closer monitoring, further assessment, or referral to a GP, sports physician or another health professional. Some athletes will simply receive a baseline and reassurance that they can continue training, with a plan to review if anything changes.

A screening result is most useful when the athlete understands what it means, what action is recommended and who will follow up. For young people, explanations and decisions should involve the athlete as well as their parent or guardian, in a way that suits their age and preferences.

Can screening predict or prevent injury?

Screening may identify issues that deserve attention, but it cannot forecast an individual injury with certainty. Research reviews have found that commonly used movement screens and isolated physical tests do not accurately predict future sports injuries well enough to be used as stand-alone injury prediction tools. A score should not be used by itself to exclude an athlete, label them “high risk” or make return-to-sport decisions.

Screening can still support care when it leads to an appropriate action—for example, addressing a relevant strength deficit, responding to symptoms, reviewing training and recovery, or referring for medical assessment. Injury prevention is better approached through ongoing, sport-appropriate strategies than through a one-off test.

When should an athlete seek further medical advice?

An athlete should stop exercise and seek prompt medical assessment for concerning symptoms such as chest pain, fainting or near-fainting during exercise, unexplained marked breathlessness, or a sudden significant change in health. New or worsening pain, recurrent injury, persistent fatigue, repeated illness or a notable change in performance also deserves discussion with a health professional.

A practical approach at Strides Performance

At Strides Performance, athlete screening is tailored to the person and the sport. It can combine a physiotherapy history and examination with selected physical tests. Where indicated, VALD ForceDecks and Dynamo+ support objective assessment of selected jump performance, force production and strength measures. Results are interpreted alongside the athlete’s symptoms, history, development and sport demands, then translated into clear priorities for training, rehabilitation or referral. For adolescent athletes, the assessment considers development, sport demands and the athlete’s own goals.

Screening is not a requirement to participate in sport and does not guarantee that injury will be prevented. It is one way to make informed decisions and give an athlete a clearer, more individualised plan.

Would an athlete screening appointment be useful?

An assessment may be useful if you are starting a new training phase, returning from injury, experiencing recurring problems, or want a clearer picture of physical capacity and next steps. To discuss an appointment with Strides Performance in Narre Warren, contact Strides Health Group on 0411 876 851 or admin@strideshealth.com.au.

Daniel Smit
Little Strides Physiotherapy

If your child is taking longer to learn movement skills, it can be difficult to know whether to wait or seek advice. Children develop at different rates, but concerns about movement or everyday participation are worth discussing with a health professional.

This guide explains what developmental delay means, how paediatric physiotherapy may help and what families can expect from an assessment. The aim is to understand your child’s needs and support meaningful progress, with goals that matter to your family.

What developmental delay means

Developmental delay describes skills developing more slowly than expected. It can affect movement, communication, learning, social interaction or everyday activities. A delay in movement does not automatically mean that other areas are affected, and the term does not by itself explain the cause. [1]

Gross motor skills involve whole-body movement, including rolling, sitting, moving between positions, standing, walking, running and jumping. Physiotherapy focuses on these skills and how a child uses them in daily life.

Some children need support across several areas. Your GP or paediatrician can consider your child’s overall development and whether further assessment is needed. Occupational therapists and speech pathologists may also contribute, depending on your child’s needs. [2]

When to seek advice

Speak with your maternal and child health nurse, GP or paediatric physiotherapist if you are concerned about progress. You do not need to wait until a difficulty becomes severe to ask questions. Examples of concerns include:

Taking longer than expected to develop sitting, standing or walking skills.

Consistently using one side of the body much more than the other.

Difficulty changing positions, managing steps or joining in age-appropriate play.

Frequent falls that interfere with activities or seem unusual for the child’s stage of development.

Milestones are a guide rather than a diagnosis. An assessment considers the pattern of development, the child’s opportunities to practise and their medical history. For children born prematurely, corrected age may be relevant when interpreting early milestones.

Loss of skills needs medical review

Losing a skill that your child previously used consistently is different from taking longer to learn a new skill. Arrange prompt medical review if your child loses movement, communication or other established skills. Seek urgent medical care for sudden weakness, a sudden inability to walk or a change accompanied by serious illness. [3, 4]

What happens at a physiotherapy assessment

The appointment starts with your concerns, your child’s history and the activities that are important to your family. This may include information about pregnancy and birth, health conditions, previous assessments and progress over time.

Through play and observation, the physiotherapist considers how your child moves, changes position, balances and explores. Depending on their age and presentation, the assessment may include strength, joint movement, posture, muscle tone and a standardised developmental measure.

You can bring your child’s health record, relevant reports and short videos showing movements that are difficult to observe in the clinic. There is no need to practise or coach your child for the assessment.

How physiotherapy may help

A plan is built around your child’s current abilities and practical goals. For one child, a goal might be moving into sitting to play; for another, it might be stepping up at kinder or joining a playground game.

Activities may involve practising a movement in smaller steps, developing strength and balance, or adjusting the task or environment so the child can participate. Where appropriate, equipment or positioning support may help. Active practice and opportunities to use skills in everyday routines are important principles of care. [5]

Muscle tone and strength are different. Some children have lower or higher muscle tone, which needs to be understood in the context of their overall presentation. Physiotherapy can support movement and function; exercise does not necessarily change the underlying muscle tone.

A child’s preferences, comfort and responses to their surroundings are considered when planning activities. If sensory responses make play or daily routines difficult, an occupational therapist may help assess these concerns and plan support with your child’s wider team.

Supporting movement at home

Home activities should be realistic and enjoyable. Rather than adding a demanding exercise routine, your physiotherapist can help you find opportunities within play and daily life. For example, reaching for a favourite toy or practising a step may support an agreed goal when the task is appropriate for your child.

Follow your child’s interests and allow time to try movements for themselves.

Use short, manageable opportunities to practise and adapt them if your child becomes tired or distressed.

Share what is working, what is difficult and what fits your family’s routine.

Ask for advice before introducing exercises or equipment if your child has pain, unusual stiffness or other medical concerns.

Progress may include easier participation, greater confidence or needing less assistance, as well as learning new skills. Reviewing goals helps keep support relevant as your child’s needs change.

Working with your child’s wider team

Developmental support may involve your GP, paediatrician, therapists, maternal and child health nurse and educators. With your consent, communication between these professionals can help coordinate goals and reduce conflicting advice. Medical assessment and therapy have complementary roles.

The type and amount of support depend on the child and the reasons for their difficulties. Your treating professionals can discuss what progress might look like and review the plan over time.

Support through Little Strides

Little Strides Physiotherapy provides paediatric physiotherapy at Strides Health Group in Narre Warren. Daniel Smit is an APA Titled Paediatric Physiotherapist who works with children and families to understand movement concerns and develop practical, individualised plans.

If you would like advice about your child’s movement or gross motor development, contact the clinic or  book a paediatric physiotherapy appointmen t. For concerns about development across several areas, speak with your GP or maternal and child health nurse as well.

[SOURCE: https://strideshealth.com.au/resources/coordination-and-motor-skills-in-children]
Every child develops movement skills in their own way

Children learn to move, play and manage everyday tasks at different rates. If your child finds some movement skills harder, less coordinated or more tiring than expected, an assessment can help you understand what is getting in the way and what support may be useful. Physiotherapy focuses on practical goals that matter to your child and family.

A child may have difficulty with one skill and be confident in another. These differences do not, on their own, mean that a child has a developmental disorder.

What are coordination and motor skills

Motor skills are the movements we use to take part in everyday life. Gross motor skills use the larger muscles for actions such as sitting, walking, running, jumping, climbing, balancing and playing with a ball. Fine motor skills use the hands and fingers for tasks such as drawing, using cutlery, fastening clothes and handling classroom tools.

Coordination includes organising and timing movements, using both sides of the body together, adjusting balance and learning a new movement sequence. Some children also need more time or practice to work out how to begin and complete an unfamiliar task.

What might families notice

A child who is finding movement skills difficult may trip or bump into things often, find playground or ball skills challenging, take longer to learn tasks such as riding a bike or using stairs, or need extra help with dressing and classroom activities. A task may take a lot of effort, and the child may tire, become frustrated or avoid it.

These examples are not a checklist for diagnosis. Consider whether the difficulty is persistent, how it affects your child’s daily life, and what your child thinks about it. A child’s interests, strengths, environment and opportunities to practise all matter.

When to consider an assessment

You can seek advice if movement difficulties continue over time, affect play, sport, self-care or learning, or make it hard for your child to join in with activities they want to do. You do not need to wait for a diagnosis before asking for support.

Start with your GP, child and family health nurse or paediatrician. A physiotherapist can assess movement, balance, coordination and gross motor skills. An occupational therapist can assess hand skills and everyday tasks such as dressing, handwriting and using tools. These professionals may work together with your family, school or early learning service.

Developmental coordination disorder (DCD), sometimes called dyspraxia, is one possible explanation for ongoing motor difficulties. A diagnosis requires a broader assessment of motor skills and their effect on everyday activities, while also considering other possible explanations. It is not made from one observation or a single sign. Movement skills vary widely in children under five, so a DCD diagnosis may be difficult at this age; concerns can still be discussed and support can still begin. [1, 5, 6]

Seek timely medical advice if your child loses skills they had already gained, develops new weakness or a marked difference between sides, or has a sudden change in movement. [7]

Physiotherapy begins by listening to your concerns and learning what your child wants or needs to do. The physiotherapist may ask about development, health, daily routines and previous opportunities to practise, then observe movement through play and relevant tasks. Standardised motor assessments may be used when appropriate. The assessment is used to understand your child’s strengths and needs, not to define them by a score.

Support is individualised. It may include choosing a meaningful goal, practising the actual skill in manageable steps, adjusting the task to make it achievable, and gradually changing the challenge as your child learns. Examples include working towards confident stair use, joining a playground game, catching a ball or learning a new movement sequence. Strength, balance and other movement exercises may also be included when they relate to the child’s goals.

The family and child help choose priorities. With your permission, strategies can be shared with educators, teachers or other therapists so your child has consistent support in the places where the skill is needed. [1, 2]

Ideas to support movement at home

Follow your child’s interests. Choose games and activities they enjoy, such as obstacle courses, playground play, dancing, ball games, drawing or helping with everyday jobs.

Practise one useful skill at a time. Break it into small steps, show the movement, and give your child time to try it.

Make the task achievable. Start with a larger ball, a lower step, a stable surface or fewer instructions, then adjust the challenge gradually.

Notice effort and problem-solving. Avoid comparing your child with siblings or classmates; progress may look different from one skill to another.

Keep practice positive and flexible. Short, regular opportunities can fit into play and routines. Pause or adapt the activity if your child is tired, uncomfortable or losing interest.

What current evidence tells us

For children with DCD, recent reviews find that motor-based interventions can improve measured motor skills and performance of activities. Practice focused on meaningful tasks is one supported approach. Evidence is less settled for longer-term effects on participation and psychosocial outcomes, and studies vary in the children included and the interventions used. This is why goals should be individual, functional and reviewed with the child and family. [3, 4]

The evidence does not mean every child needs therapy or that one approach suits everyone. An assessment can help decide whether physiotherapy, occupational therapy, medical review, environmental changes, or a combination would best support your child.

[SOURCE: https://strideshealth.com.au/resources/gross-motor-development-in-children]
Gross motor skills are the big body movements children use to explore, play and take part in daily life. They involve the trunk, arms and legs and include skills such as rolling, sitting, crawling or moving in other ways, walking, running, climbing, jumping and balancing.

Children develop movement skills in a broad sequence, but the timing and the way a skill looks can vary. Some children skip particular ways of moving, use mobility aids, or develop skills in a different order. A milestone list is a guide for noticing development, not a test or diagnosis. If you are concerned, you know your child best and it is appropriate to ask for advice.

How gross motor skills develop

The following are broad examples of skills that may emerge over time. They are not strict age deadlines, and each skill depends on a child’s opportunities, health, preferences and individual development.

Babies: building control and exploring movement

Babies gradually develop head and trunk control, reach and turn, roll, sit, and find ways to move across the floor. Some crawl on hands and knees; others shuffle, roll, commando crawl or move in another way. Later in the first year, many begin pulling to stand and cruising along furniture.

Toddlers: getting around and trying new challenges

As children become more mobile, they practise walking, squatting to pick something up, getting back to standing, carrying objects while moving, climbing onto low furniture and navigating steps with support. Walking may be unsteady at first, and falls are common while the skill is new.

Preschoolers: combining skills

Children build confidence and coordination through running, changing direction, kicking and throwing a ball, climbing, jumping, balancing and using stairs. Some may begin hopping, pedalling or catching a large ball as these skills become familiar.

School-age children: refining movement and participation

Strength, balance, timing and coordination continue to develop as children learn more complex movement skills and join in games, physical education, recreation and sport. Interests and experience shape which skills they practise.

Play that supports movement

Children learn through active, enjoyable experiences rather than drills. Everyday play gives them chances to practise a movement, adapt it and try again. Follow your child’s interests and make the activity easier or harder as needed.

For babies: offer supervised floor play while awake, including short, comfortable periods on the tummy; place a toy or familiar face nearby to encourage looking, reaching and turning. Australian guidance recommends building toward at least 30 minutes of tummy time spread across the day for babies who are not yet mobile.

For toddlers: make space for cruising, walking between safe supports, carrying toys, stepping over low soft obstacles, climbing at a suitable playground and playing with push-along toys.

For preschoolers: try dancing, animal walks, bubbles, ball games, playground play, nature walks, stepping stones, jumping over chalk lines or riding a tricycle when ready.

For all children: include accessible options, adapt equipment and surfaces, and allow rest. The goal is enjoyable participation, not performing a movement in one prescribed way.

Talk with your GP, child and family health nurse, paediatrician or paediatric physiotherapist if your child is not gaining movement skills over time, has difficulty joining everyday play, seems to use one side much less than the other, appears unusually stiff or floppy, or you have any other concern. Seek advice promptly if your child loses a skill they had previously gained. You do not need to wait for a particular age or for a concern to become severe before asking.

A physiotherapy assessment looks at your child’s movement in context: what they enjoy, what is hard, how they move, and what would help them take part. Support may include practical play ideas, adapting an activity or environment, and working with your family and other professionals around goals that matter to you.

A note about development

This page offers general information and cannot assess an individual child. Children born prematurely may be followed using corrected age in early development; discuss what is appropriate for your child with their health professional. The Australian 24-hour movement guidelines recommend plenty of floor play for babies under one year, and at least three hours of physical activity spread through the day for children aged one to five years, with energetic play included.

How physiotherapy can help with a head-turning preference

A persistent head tilt or preference to look one way is worth checking

Some babies consistently tilt their head to one side, turn more easily in one direction or find feeding more comfortable on one side. This may be related to congenital muscular torticollis, a condition involving tightness or shortening of a neck muscle, or a postural preference without clear muscle tightness. An assessment can help clarify what is contributing and whether support is needed.

Torticollis describes a head and neck position; it is not a diagnosis by itself. A new or sudden head tilt, especially when your baby is unwell or in pain, needs prompt medical advice because causes other than congenital muscular torticollis must be considered.

What you might notice

Your baby often tilts their head towards the same shoulder, while looking or turning towards the other side.

They seem to have an easier direction for looking, or become unsettled when positioned to look the other way.

They prefer feeding on one side or have difficulty comfortably turning towards one breast or bottle position.

There is a developing flat area on one side or at the back of the head, or the face looks slightly asymmetrical.

They use one side of the body more, or movement during play looks less even.

These signs do not confirm torticollis. Some babies have a positional preference without muscle restriction, and head shape differences can have more than one contributor. Your GP, child and family health nurse or paediatric physiotherapist can assess your baby and guide next steps.

How paediatric physiotherapy can help

A paediatric physiotherapist looks at your baby’s whole movement pattern, not only the neck. The assessment may include preferred head posture, comfortable active and passive neck movement, neck muscle length and strength, head and facial symmetry, trunk and limb movement, early motor skills, feeding or positioning concerns, and signs that another health professional should assess. Your GP may also consider hip screening, as hip dysplasia can occur alongside congenital muscular torticollis.

Care is individualised to your baby and family. It may include:

gentle, clinician-guided activities to support comfortable neck movement and strength

play that encourages looking and moving in both directions and using both sides of the body

practical positioning ideas for feeding, carrying, floor play and daily routines

caregiver coaching and a simple home plan that fits your baby’s cues and tolerance

monitoring of head shape, symmetry and developmental movement, with referral or collaboration when needed.

Physiotherapy is most useful when it begins early if a movement preference or restriction is present. The plan and expected progress vary from baby to baby; your physiotherapist will review movement, function and your goals over time.

Safe positioning and play at home

Everyday routines can provide chances to practise looking both ways. Follow your baby’s cues and choose positions that are comfortable and safe.

Offer supervised tummy time while your baby is awake, using short, frequent opportunities if that suits them. Stay within arm’s reach.

During awake play, place your face, voice or a toy on the less-preferred side to invite (not force) your baby to look that way.

Vary the side you hold or carry your baby on, and alternate the direction you approach from during feeds and care when practical.

Build movement opportunities into floor play and limit long stretches in car seats, capsules, swings and other containers when they are not needed for travel or care.

For every sleep, place your baby on their back on a firm, flat, clear sleep surface. Do not use pillows, wedges, positioners or rolled towels to hold the head in place.

Do not pull or force your baby’s neck into a stretch. Ask your physiotherapist to demonstrate any hands-on technique and show you how to adapt it to your baby.

When to seek medical advice

Contact your GP or child and family health nurse if you notice a persistent head tilt, reduced turning to one side, increasing head-shape asymmetry, a neck lump, or difficulty with feeding or movement. Arrange an early paediatric physiotherapy assessment if the preference persists or movement seems restricted.

Seek prompt medical assessment for a sudden or painful head tilt, a tilt after a fall or injury, fever or appearing unwell, difficulty swallowing or unusual drooling, severe headache, repeated vomiting, unusual eye movements or vision changes, poor balance, weakness, unusual sleepiness, or other new neurological symptoms. In an emergency call 000.

[SOURCE: https://strideshealth.com.au/resources/physiotherapy-for-children-with-autism]
Children with autism have individual strengths, interests, preferences and support needs. Some children also experience differences in balance, coordination, motor planning, strength, stamina or movement skills. These differences do not look the same for every child, and autism itself does not mean a child needs physiotherapy.

When movement differences make everyday activities, play, sport, school routines or community participation harder, paediatric physiotherapy can help identify practical goals and build skills in ways that fit the child and family.

How movement can affect everyday life

Movement skills support many parts of a child’s day: getting up and down from the floor, managing steps, running and jumping, joining playground games, learning to ride a scooter or bike, swimming, dressing and keeping up with peers. A child may have difficulty with one movement area and be confident in others.

Research suggests motor differences are common among children with autism, but prevalence estimates vary. Recent studies also report that structured exercise and sport programmes can improve some measured motor skills. The results are encouraging, although the studies are generally small and use different programmes and outcome measures. More research is needed to understand which approaches work best for which children and whether measured gains carry over into everyday participation. [1,2]

What paediatric physiotherapy may support

A physiotherapist can assess how movement is working for your child and what matters most to them and your family. Depending on the child’s goals, support may focus on:

balance, coordination and motor planning

strength, mobility and stamina for daily activities

walking, running, jumping, stairs and getting up from the floor

gross motor skills for playground activities, swimming, cycling or sport

confidence and access to movement that the child enjoys

adapting activities, equipment or the environment to make participation easier.

Goals are chosen with the child and family. Progress can be measured in a way that is meaningful in real life – for example, stepping onto a kerb more independently, joining a preferred game, or feeling more confident at the pool.

What an assessment may involve

An initial appointment usually includes a conversation about your child’s interests, strengths, daily routines, movement concerns and priorities. The physiotherapist may observe or assess selected activities such as walking, balance, coordination, strength or functional movement. Assessment should be paced to suit the child, with clear explanations, breaks and adjustments where helpful.

A plan is developed together and reviewed over time. It may include play-based practice, activity ideas for home or community settings, advice for school or sport, and communication with other members of the child’s support team when the family wants this.

Making movement activities work for your child

Enjoyment, choice and a sense of success can help children stay involved in physical activity. Research with autistic young people also highlights the importance of feeling capable and the impact that sensory features of an activity setting can have on participation. [3] A physiotherapist can help families and activity providers consider options such as:

starting with the child’s interests and offering meaningful choices

using a predictable routine, visual cues or a clear demonstration when useful

adjusting noise, lighting, clothing, temperature, waiting time or group size to the child’s preferences

breaking a new movement into manageable steps and building practice gradually

choosing a quieter time or a familiar place to try an activity

valuing enjoyment, comfort and participation alongside skill development.

There is no single activity that suits every child. Swimming, dancing, playground play, walking, martial arts, cycling and ball games may all be options, depending on the child’s preferences, access and goals. Recent reviews report potential motor benefits from structured programmes, including organised sport, but do not establish one best programme for every child. [1,2]

A note about W-sitting

W-sitting is not automatically harmful and does not need to be corrected simply because a child uses it. It can be one of several comfortable sitting positions. Consider assessment if it is the child’s only comfortable position, or if it occurs alongside pain, stiffness, marked asymmetry, frequent falls, unusual walking or concerns about motor development. The goal is to support comfort and a range of movement options, not to police one posture.

When to seek a physiotherapy assessment

You may wish to speak with a paediatric physiotherapist if movement differences:

make everyday tasks or moving around less independent

lead to repeated falls or difficulty keeping up with activities

limit access to play, sport, school or community activities the child wants to join

are associated with pain or a noticeable change in walking or movement.

Sudden weakness, loss of previously acquired skills, persistent or night pain, swelling, or an acute change in walking should be discussed promptly with a medical practitioner.

Working with your child and family

Support should respect the child’s communication, preferences and sensory needs. Language preferences vary: some people prefer identity-first language (“autistic child”), while others prefer person-first language (“child with autism”). We use “children with autism” here and are happy to follow the language each child and family prefers.

The Australian National Guideline for supporting autistic children and their families emphasises supports that are safe, effective and desirable to children and families, with attention to goals, planning, delivery and review. [4] Physiotherapy is one part of a broader support network and can be coordinated with other professionals when appropriate.

A flat area on a baby’s head can be worrying to notice. Positional plagiocephaly is common and usually improves as babies grow and move more. A paediatric physiotherapist can check your baby’s head and neck movement, look for a preferred position or neck tightness, and show you practical ways to encourage comfortable movement while your baby is awake. Safe sleep always comes first.

What is positional plagiocephaly?

Positional plagiocephaly is flattening on one side at the back of a baby’s head. Brachycephaly is flattening across the back of the head, which can make it look wider. These shapes can occur when pressure rests on the same part of a young, soft skull for repeated periods. Some babies have head-shape changes from their position before or during birth; others develop them after birth. Birth moulding often settles over time. [1, 2]

Does it affect my baby’s brain or development?

Positional plagiocephaly changes the outside shape of the head and does not affect brain growth. It is not known to cause developmental delay. If you have concerns about your baby’s movement or development, discuss them with your maternal and child health nurse, GP or paediatrician. Head shape alone cannot tell you how a baby is developing. [1, 2]

Why might a flat spot develop?

A baby may spend more time looking or resting in one direction. Neck tightness, called torticollis, can make it harder to turn the head both ways. Limited movement for other health reasons can also mean the head rests in one position for longer. This is not caused by a parent doing something wrong. Back sleeping remains the safest sleep position; the aim is to offer varied movement and positions during awake time. [1, 2]

How can paediatric physiotherapy help?

A paediatric physiotherapist will ask about your baby’s birth, health, daily positions and movement. They may check head shape, head-turning preference, neck movement, head tilt, posture, head control and early motor skills. They can also look for signs that another medical assessment is needed.

Your physiotherapist can then make a plan that fits your baby and family. This may include:

ways to arrange play and the room so your baby has reasons to look both ways;

comfortable carrying, holding and feeding positions that vary the direction your baby looks;

short, frequent periods of supervised tummy time and other awake movement;

specific neck movement or stretching activities if your baby has restricted movement or torticollis; and

follow-up to check movement and head shape and discuss whether a medical or specialist referral is appropriate.

What physiotherapy can and cannot do

Only use activities shown by your physiotherapist; never force your baby’s head or neck. Physiotherapy supports movement and reduces repeated pressure while your baby is awake; it does not directly reshape the skull. The 2024 APTA guideline supports an individualised physiotherapy plan for torticollis specifically, rather than head shape alone. [3]

What can I do at home?

Keep sleep safe

Always place your baby on their back to sleep, on a firm, flat mattress in a clear cot.

Do not use pillows, wedges, head-shaping pillows, positioners or rolled towels in the cot.

You can alternate which end of the cot your baby’s head is placed at when putting them down, so they have different things to look towards. You do not need to keep turning a sleeping baby. [1, 2]

Vary positions while your baby is awake

Offer short, frequent tummy-time sessions while your baby is awake and supervised. Start with what your baby can manage and build gradually.

Use your face, voice or a toy on both sides to encourage your baby to turn their head.

Vary holding and feeding sides where practical. Give your baby opportunities for floor play and movement in different comfortable positions.

What does current research say?

A 2025 systematic review found that early, clinician-supported guidance for parents may help prevent infant asymmetry. The studies differed too much to combine, and the authors rated the overall evidence very low. This supports offering practical advice early while being honest that the best programme and its effects are not yet certain. [4]

Most babies do not need a helmet. A specialist may discuss helmet therapy when flattening is severe and persists despite appropriate advice and physiotherapy. If you are concerned about your baby’s head shape, ask early so that assessment and referral can be considered in good time. The decision depends on the degree of flattening, your baby’s age and progress, neck movement and family circumstances. [1, 2]

Research findings are mixed. A 2025 meta-analysis of 13 studies involving 1,189 infants found greater short-term improvement in head-shape measures in the helmet groups. However, results varied greatly between studies, and the authors called for better randomised trials and longer-term evidence. A 2026 systematic review also suggested helmets may speed early correction in some moderate-to-severe cases, but highlighted that much of the evidence is observational and does not establish clear long-term superiority. [5, 6]

In one randomised trial, 84 selected infants with moderate-to-severe skull deformation were assigned to helmet treatment or natural improvement. The researchers found no significant difference in head shape at two years. This trial does not answer every clinical question, including what is best for very severe cases or babies with torticollis. Taken together, the evidence supports an individual discussion with a clinician rather than routine helmet use for every baby with a flat spot. [7]

When should I ask for medical advice?

Talk with your maternal and child health nurse, GP, paediatrician or paediatric physiotherapist if your baby usually looks one way, has a head tilt, has difficulty turning their head both ways, or you notice flattening or facial or ear asymmetry. Seek prompt medical review if the head shape was unusual from birth, is progressively worsening, or you notice a firm ridge along a skull suture. These signs can have causes other than positional flattening, including craniosynostosis, and need clinical assessment. Imaging is not usually needed to diagnose positional plagiocephaly; a clinician will decide if further tests or specialist review are indicated. [1, 2]

Frequently asked questions

Should I use a pillow to protect the flat spot?

No. Do not use pillows or positioning devices in the cot. Always place your baby on their back to sleep. Use awake time for supervised tummy time, play and varied comfortable positions. [1, 2]

Will the flat spot go away on its own?

Many babies improve as they grow, gain head control and move more. The amount of change varies. A clinician can monitor progress and help identify neck tightness or other factors that may need attention. [1, 2]

Does every baby with a flat spot need physiotherapy?

Not always. Many babies need reassurance and practical positioning advice only. Physiotherapy can be especially useful when a baby has a strong head-turning preference, neck tightness, torticollis or difficulty moving symmetrically. Your health professional can help decide what is appropriate.

Knowing when to seek help
Daniel Smit

Back pain can make work, sleep and everyday movement difficult. For many adults, the most useful starting point is an assessment when needed, clear advice and a gradual return to activities that matter. Prolonged bed rest is usually unhelpful.

This guide focuses on low back pain in adults. It explains practical steps, the role of physiotherapy and symptoms that need urgent care. Advice for a new episode can differ from support for pain that has persisted for months.

Understanding low back pain

Low back pain is felt in the lower part of the back and may occur with leg symptoms. Many episodes are described as non-specific: assessment does not identify a single disease or injury that explains the pain. This does not make the pain less real.

Pain intensity does not reliably measure the amount of tissue damage. Many new episodes improve over the following weeks, although recovery varies and recurrences are common. An assessment helps check for conditions that need a different approach. [1]

Keep moving with sensible adjustments

During a new episode, try to continue manageable everyday activities. You might shorten a walk, reduce a load or change how long you spend on a task. Brief rest can be useful, but spending extended periods in bed generally does not support recovery.

A 2025 Cochrane overview found that advice to stay active probably reduces short-term pain compared with advice to rest in bed for acute or subacute low back pain. This supports adapting activity rather than waiting for every symptom to disappear. [2]

For example, several short walks may feel more manageable than one long walk. Choose a starting point suited to your symptoms and health, then increase gradually. Seek advice if activity causes a substantial ongoing worsening or new leg symptoms.

Do you need a scan

An X-ray or MRI is not routinely needed for uncomplicated low back pain. Imaging is considered when the history or examination suggests serious disease, or when it is likely to change treatment decisions. Common age-related changes on scans may also be present in people without pain, so findings need to be interpreted alongside your symptoms. [1]

Exercise when pain persists

When pain lasts more than three months, a structured exercise programme can be part of care. Research supports exercise for persistent non-specific low back pain, but benefits vary and improvements are often modest. A programme should fit your abilities, preferences and goals. [2, 3]

Walking, strengthening, water-based activity or other exercise may be appropriate. The useful question is which activities you can practise consistently and progress, rather than searching for a single exercise that will fix every back problem.

A new painful episode does not automatically require a demanding strengthening programme. Start with an appropriate level of movement and review the plan as symptoms and function change.

Walking after recovery may reduce recurrence

The Australian WalkBack trial, published in The Lancet in 2024, studied adults who had recently recovered from non-specific low back pain. An individualised, progressive walking programme combined with education and physiotherapist support delayed the return of activity-limiting back pain compared with no intervention. [4]

The result supports building a sustainable activity routine after recovery. It does not show that walking alone has the same effect, or that everyone should immediately walk for a fixed duration during a painful flare-up. The programme was tailored, and some participants experienced lower-limb problems, reinforcing the value of gradual progression. [4]

What about hands on treatment

Hands-on treatment may help some people manage symptoms and feel more comfortable moving. For persistent primary low back pain, WHO recommends considering a personalised combination of education, exercise and selected other treatments, rather than relying on one intervention in isolation. [3]

Treatment should be reviewed against practical outcomes: walking further, sleeping more comfortably, returning to work or lifting with confidence. Discuss the likely benefits, costs and alternatives with your treating professional.

Consider the whole picture

Sleep, stress, mood, other health conditions and worries about movement can influence the experience of persistent pain. Addressing these factors alongside physical activity is part of care; it does not mean the pain is imagined. Some people benefit from coordinated input from a GP, physiotherapist or psychologist. [3]

If you are considering pain medicines, ask your GP or pharmacist about the potential benefits, risks and suitability for you. Medicines should support a broader management plan. [1]

When to seek assessment

Arrange a review if pain is worsening, repeatedly returning, not improving or limiting work, sleep and daily activities. Leg pain, numbness or weakness also warrant assessment. You do not need to wait three months to seek help.

Seek emergency medical care for new symptoms with back pain such as:

Difficulty passing urine or loss of bladder or bowel control.

Numbness around the genitals, buttocks or inner thighs, sometimes called saddle numbness.

Severe or rapidly worsening leg weakness.

Seek prompt medical review for back pain with fever or feeling significantly unwell, following a significant injury, or with unexplained weight loss or a history of cancer. Mention osteoporosis or prolonged steroid use, particularly if pain followed a fall or minor injury. These features do not establish a diagnosis, but need clinical assessment. [1]

How physiotherapy can help

Shoulder pain is common and often manageable

Shoulder pain can make everyday tasks such as dressing, reaching overhead, lifting, working and sleeping more difficult. It may begin after a specific injury or build gradually with changes in activity. Many people improve with a clear explanation, sensible adjustments to activity and a progressive exercise plan.

The shoulder works with the neck, upper back, shoulder blade and arm. Pain in this area can come from different tissues or may be referred from the neck or, less commonly, another part of the body. An assessment helps identify what is most relevant for you.

What might be causing it

Common contributors include rotator cuff related pain, tendon or bursa irritation, a sudden muscle or tendon injury, shoulder stiffness such as frozen shoulder, arthritis, or pain referred from the neck. A fall or direct impact can also affect the joint, collarbone or surrounding soft tissues. These problems can feel similar, so symptoms alone cannot confirm a specific diagnosis.

Understanding pain and scan results

Pain does not always mean that the shoulder is being damaged each time it hurts. Sensitivity can rise when the shoulder is asked to do more than it is currently prepared for, after a change in routine, or during a flare-up. Gradually rebuilding movement and strength can help restore confidence and capacity.

Scans are not needed for every episode of shoulder pain. A physiotherapist or GP can assess your history and movement and decide whether imaging or medical review is likely to change the plan. Imaging can show age-related changes that are also present in people without pain, so results need to be interpreted alongside your symptoms and examination.

What you can do now

Keep the shoulder moving.
Continue comfortable daily movement and avoid prolonged complete rest where possible.

Adjust, then rebuild.
Temporarily reduce or modify activities that cause a marked flare, such as repeated heavy lifting or long periods overhead. Reintroduce them gradually as symptoms settle.

Find a comfortable starting point.
Gentle supported movement or light strengthening may be useful. The right exercise and amount depend on the cause, irritability and your goals.

Make sleep easier.
Try sleeping on the other side or on your back with the sore arm supported by a pillow. Change position if it becomes uncomfortable.

Use pain as information.
A small, short-lived increase during or after activity can happen. Reduce the range, resistance or repetitions if pain becomes sharp, continues to build, or remains substantially worse afterwards.

Your physiotherapist will ask how the pain began, what affects it, how it is changing and which activities you want to return to. Assessment may include shoulder and neck movement, strength, relevant neurological checks and task-specific movements. The findings guide whether further medical review or imaging is appropriate.

Care is individualised. It may include education, activity and load planning, progressive movement and strengthening, strategies to build confidence with daily tasks, and hands-on treatment when useful for short-term symptom relief. Current clinical guidance supports an active rehabilitation exercise program for rotator cuff related shoulder pain, alongside patient-centred education and self-management.

When to seek medical help

Call 000 for shoulder or arm pain with chest tightness or pain, shortness of breath, faintness or other symptoms that could indicate a heart or lung emergency. Seek urgent medical assessment after a significant injury if the shoulder looks deformed, you cannot use the arm, or there is marked swelling or severe pain. Prompt medical review is also important if the shoulder is hot, red and swollen with fever, or you develop new or worsening numbness or weakness.

Arrange a GP or physiotherapy assessment if pain is not improving over a few weeks, is limiting sleep or daily activities, follows an injury, or you are unsure what to do. A GP can review medical causes and discuss medication or imaging where appropriate.

Do I need a scan?

Not always. Many shoulder problems can be assessed clinically, and scans are most useful when the result is expected to guide a decision. Your physiotherapist or GP can help determine whether imaging is appropriate.

Should I stop exercising?

Usually, you can remain active with temporary changes to the movements or loads that aggravate symptoms. Complete rest can reduce capacity. A gradual return is generally more useful than waiting until every symptom has disappeared.

How long will it take?

Recovery varies with the cause, how long symptoms have been present, other health factors and the demands you want to return to. Progress may take weeks or longer. Review the plan if symptoms are worsening or not changing as expected.

Balance can change at any age

Balance helps us stay steady when we stand, walk, turn, reach and move across different surfaces. It depends on the brain combining information from vision, the inner ear, sensation in the feet and joints, and the body’s strength and movement responses. A change in any of these systems can make everyday movement feel less secure.

A fall is not an inevitable part of ageing. Falls can happen at any age, and they often have more than one contributing factor. A fall, near-fall, new unsteadiness or growing concern about falling is a useful reason to check what may be affecting movement and confidence.

What can affect balance and falls risk?

Balance and falls risk can be influenced by a combination of health, movement and environmental factors, including:

reduced leg strength, endurance, walking speed or confidence after illness, injury or a period of reduced activity

dizziness, vertigo or difficulty adapting to head movement and changes in position

changes in vision, sensation in the feet, joint awareness or hearing

neurological or other health conditions that affect coordination, movement or reaction time

pain, fatigue, an acute illness, dehydration or feeling faint when standing

medicines that may contribute to dizziness, drowsiness or changes in blood pressure; ask your prescriber or pharmacist to review these, and do not stop a medicine without advice

foot pain, unsuitable footwear, mobility aids that need adjustment, or hazards such as poor lighting, loose rugs and clutter.

When should you seek an assessment?

Consider speaking with your GP or physiotherapist if you have fallen, had near-falls, feel unsteady, have new dizziness, find it harder to get up from a chair or manage stairs, or are avoiding activities because you are worried about falling. A fall can still be worth discussing even if you were not injured.

Seek urgent medical help after a fall if there may be a serious injury, you have hit your head and feel unwell, you cannot get up, you have severe pain, fainting, chest pain, new weakness or numbness, difficulty speaking, or a sudden severe headache. Call 000 for an emergency. If you may be injured, do not try to stand without help.

A physiotherapist can ask about your falls, near-falls, dizziness, health, medicines, daily activities and goals, then assess relevant aspects of strength, balance, walking, transfers and movement. The assessment helps identify what is most relevant for you and whether input from your GP, pharmacist, occupational therapist, optometrist, podiatrist or another health professional would also be useful.

Support may include an individual exercise plan, practice of everyday tasks such as standing from a chair or turning while walking, balance and lower-limb strength training, advice about safe mobility, and vestibular rehabilitation when indicated and within the clinician’s scope. Exercises can be adapted for different abilities and progressed as capacity changes.

Practical steps that may help

Keep moving in ways that feel manageable. Build activity gradually and choose activities that fit your health and goals.

Include exercises that challenge balance and everyday movement, alongside strength work. Walking is beneficial for health, but walking alone may not provide enough balance challenge for falls prevention.

For older adults, Australian community guidelines recommend 2–3 hours of ongoing exercise each week, primarily targeting balance and mobility and including strength training. The amount and starting level should be appropriate to the person; people with previous falls or significant balance difficulties may need a tailored, supervised plan.

Wear secure, well-fitting footwear and use a prescribed mobility aid as advised. Ask a clinician to check the fit and show you how to use it safely.

Improve lighting, keep frequently used pathways clear, secure loose mats and arrange rails or other home changes to match your needs. An occupational therapist can provide a tailored home safety assessment.

Arrange vision, hearing, foot and medication reviews when relevant. Stand up gradually if you feel light-headed and discuss recurrent symptoms with a health professional.

What to do after a fall

If you are injured, have severe symptoms or cannot get up, call 000 or use your personal alarm/emergency contact. If you are on the floor and do not appear injured, take your time, keep warm and call for help if you are unsure or unable to get up safely. Contact your GP or physiotherapist after a fall to discuss what happened and whether a review would help. Recording when, where and how the fall happened can help guide that conversation.

How Strides can support you

At Strides Health Group, adult physiotherapy is centred on what matters to you. We can assess balance, walking, strength, dizziness and everyday mobility, then work with you on a practical plan that supports participation and independence. If your symptoms are sudden, severe or medically concerning, seek urgent medical care rather than waiting for a physiotherapy appointment.

Understanding symptoms and how physiotherapy may help

Dizziness can feel unsettling and can affect walking, work, exercise and confidence. It is a symptom rather than a diagnosis. The pattern of symptoms and a careful assessment help identify what may be contributing and what support is appropriate.

What does dizziness feel like?

People use “dizziness” to describe several sensations: spinning or movement (vertigo), light-headedness or feeling faint, unsteadiness, or difficulty focusing the eyes while moving. You may also notice nausea, fatigue, headache, hearing changes or ringing in the ears. These experiences can overlap, so describing them in your own words is useful.

Common patterns and possible causes

Dizziness has many possible causes. Examples include inner-ear conditions, migraine, changes in blood pressure, medication effects and other medical conditions. A physiotherapy assessment can help identify a vestibular pattern, but it cannot rule out every medical cause.

Brief spinning with position changes

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) can cause short episodes of spinning when rolling in bed, lying back, looking up or sitting up. It involves tiny inner-ear particles moving into a balance canal. Specific positional tests help confirm the pattern.

Persistent dizziness or visual blurring with head movement

A reduced inner-ear balance response can make head movement feel uncomfortable, blur vision during movement or affect balance. Vestibular rehabilitation may use individually selected gaze, movement and balance exercises.

Episodes with headache, light sensitivity or motion sensitivity

Vestibular migraine can include dizziness or vertigo, sometimes with headache and sometimes without. Medical assessment is useful, especially when symptoms are new or changing.

Light-headedness or feeling faint

Blood pressure changes, illness, dehydration, medication effects and other causes can contribute. These symptoms may need assessment by a GP or another medical professional.

Your physiotherapist will ask about what the dizziness feels like, when it began, how long episodes last, what brings them on, and any associated symptoms or relevant health history. Assessment may include eye movements, balance and walking, head movement, and—when appropriate—positional testing. If the symptoms suggest a medical cause that needs further investigation, we will recommend medical review.

Treatment depends on the findings. For confirmed BPPV, a clinician may use a canalith repositioning manoeuvre suited to the affected canal and side. For some inner-ear balance problems, an individual vestibular rehabilitation program may include eye-head coordination, graded movement, balance and walking practice. The program is adjusted to your symptoms, goals and safety. Exercises are not the same for every cause of dizziness.

What you can do while arranging assessment

Sit or lie down somewhere safe if you feel dizzy, and allow symptoms to settle before standing.

Move carefully when getting out of bed or changing position. Use support if you feel unsteady and reduce trip hazards around you.

Do not drive, cycle in traffic, climb ladders or use machinery while dizzy or if your vision or balance is affected. Ask your doctor when it is safe to resume.

Note the timing, duration, triggers and associated symptoms. Bring a list of medicines and recent changes to your appointment.

Seek emergency care now

Call 000 or go to an emergency department if dizziness occurs with sudden weakness or numbness (especially on one side), facial droop, trouble speaking or swallowing, double vision or sudden vision problems, severe trouble walking or coordinating movement, confusion, or a sudden severe headache. Do not drive yourself.

Arrange a GP assessment for new, recurring, persistent or worsening dizziness, or if you have a fall, fainting, new hearing loss, significant vomiting, or concerns about medicines. If you are unsure what to do in Victoria, call NURSE-ON-CALL on 1300 60 60 24. Call 000 for an emergency.

Physiotherapy at Strides Health Group

We provide individual physiotherapy assessment and care for dizziness and balance concerns. Your first appointment will focus on understanding your symptoms, checking whether physiotherapy is appropriate and agreeing on a safe plan. You may be referred to your GP or another health professional when needed.

Understanding your knee and finding a way forward

Knee osteoarthritis can cause pain, stiffness, swelling and difficulty with everyday activities. Symptoms can change from day to day, but there are practical ways to improve strength, confidence and function. A tailored plan starts with understanding what matters to you, staying active and building capacity over time.

The key message

A scan does not define how your knee will feel or what you can do. Exercise, education and a plan that fits your life are central parts of care.

What is knee osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis is a whole-joint condition involving changes in cartilage, bone and other joint tissues. It is common, especially as people get older, and affects people in different ways. The amount of change seen on an X-ray does not reliably predict how much pain or difficulty someone will experience.

Pain is real, but it is influenced by more than the joint image. Activity levels, muscle strength, sleep, stress, other health conditions and confidence moving can all affect symptoms. A flare does not automatically mean you have damaged your knee.

Common symptoms

People may notice pain with walking, stairs, standing from a chair or longer periods of activity; stiffness after rest or first thing in the morning; swelling; reduced strength; or a feeling that the knee is less steady. Symptoms often vary. A clinician can assess your history, movement and goals to check whether osteoarthritis is the most likely explanation or whether another condition needs attention.

How is it diagnosed

For many people aged 45 and over with a typical history and examination, knee osteoarthritis can be diagnosed clinically. Imaging is not routinely needed to confirm it or begin effective treatment. If imaging is needed because the presentation is unusual, symptoms follow an injury, or the result would change care, an X-ray is generally the first imaging test. MRI is not usually needed to diagnose osteoarthritis.

What can help

Exercise and physical activity

Regular, tailored exercise is a core treatment. A programme may combine strengthening, balance, mobility and aerobic activity such as walking, cycling or water-based exercise. The best starting point is one you can do consistently and adapt to your symptoms, preferences and access.

A physiotherapist can help set a suitable starting level, choose exercises linked to your goals and progress the plan. Some temporary discomfort when starting or progressing exercise can occur. It should settle towards your usual level after activity. If pain or swelling is noticeably worse and remains elevated into the next day, reduce the next session and build up more gradually. Seek individual advice if you are unsure.

Using GLA:D principles in individual physiotherapy

GLA:D® stands for Good Life with osteoArthritis: Denmark. GLA:D® Australia is a structured education and supervised exercise programme for hip or knee osteoarthritis. The full programme includes an initial assessment, two education sessions, 12 supervised exercise sessions and follow-up.

Daniel has completed GLA:D® Australia clinician training. Strides Health Group does not currently offer the full formal GLA:D® programme. When appropriate, relevant education and exercise principles may be incorporated into individual physiotherapy sessions and tailored to your goals. Individual physiotherapy is not the formal GLA:D® programme. Ask us about an approach that suits you.

Build activity into daily life

Try to avoid an all-or-nothing cycle of doing too much on a good day and then stopping completely. Pacing, planned breaks and gradually increasing walking or other meaningful activities can help build consistency. Choose goals that matter to you, such as getting around the shops, gardening, playing with family or returning to a hobby.

Weight and nutrition, when relevant

If you are living with higher body weight, support for gradual weight reduction may reduce pressure on the knee and improve symptoms for some people. Discuss this respectfully in the context of your health, preferences and circumstances. Exercise remains helpful at any body size. A GP or dietitian can help with a safe, sustainable approach.

Symptom relief and other supports

A GP or pharmacist can discuss medicines and other pain-relief options, including possible benefits, risks and interactions for your health situation. Some people find a walking aid useful for a period or for longer distances. Hands-on treatment may provide short-term relief for some people, but is usually most useful alongside exercise and self-management.

When should I seek advice

Arrange an assessment if knee symptoms limit daily activities, affect sleep, worsen, or make it hard to stay active. Seek urgent medical care for a suddenly hot, red, very swollen knee with fever or feeling unwell; a major injury or inability to bear weight; or new severe symptoms that are rapidly worsening.

What if symptoms remain severe

If pain and function remain substantially affected despite a well-supported course of non-surgical care, talk with your GP or treating clinician about a review and whether specialist assessment is appropriate. Joint replacement may be considered when symptoms and disability remain severe. Surgery is a decision made with you after discussing expected benefits, risks and alternatives.

A useful first step

Choose one activity that matters to you. Start at a manageable level, keep it regular and review your progress with your physiotherapist or GP.

Neck pain is common and often manageable

Neck pain can affect turning your head, driving, working, sleeping or taking part in sport and everyday activities. It may start after a sudden movement or injury, or build gradually. Pain can also spread into the shoulder or arm, and some people notice tingling, numbness or weakness. Most episodes are not caused by a serious condition and improve with time, appropriate activity and a plan that fits the person.

The neck works with the upper back, shoulders, nerves and the rest of the body. There is not always one single structure that explains symptoms. How the pain began, what affects it, your health and your daily demands all help guide an assessment.

What might contribute to neck pain

Common contributors include muscle or joint irritation, a change in activity, prolonged or unfamiliar positions, stress, poor sleep, or an injury such as whiplash. Sometimes a nerve in the neck is irritated, which can cause pain, pins and needles, altered sensation or weakness into an arm or hand. Headache can occur alongside neck pain. Less commonly, neck pain may be related to another medical condition. Symptoms alone cannot confirm a specific diagnosis.

Pain, posture and scan results

Pain is real, but it does not always mean that the neck is being damaged each time it hurts. Symptoms can become more sensitive during a flare, with fatigue or stress, after disrupted sleep, or when activity changes quickly. A sore neck does not mean you need to hold it perfectly still or maintain one “ideal” posture. Changing position regularly and building tolerance can be more useful than trying to keep one posture all day.

Scans are not needed for every episode of neck pain. A GP or physiotherapist can assess your symptoms and decide whether imaging or medical review is likely to change the plan. Scans can show age-related changes that are also present in people without pain, so results need to be interpreted alongside your symptoms and examination. Imaging may be appropriate after some injuries, when certain warning signs are present, or when symptoms are persistent or worsening.

Keep moving within a comfortable range. Gentle neck and shoulder movement and usual daily activity can help maintain confidence and mobility. Avoid prolonged complete rest where possible.

Adjust, then rebuild. Temporarily modify tasks that cause a strong or lasting flare, then gradually return to them. Consider shorter work or screen blocks, position changes and breaks that suit your day.

Use a comfortable starting point. Gentle movement, walking or light strengthening may help. The right exercises and amount depend on your symptoms and goals; an individual assessment can guide this.

Support sleep and recovery. Try a comfortable pillow and sleep position, and allow time for rest. No single pillow or sleeping position is right for everyone.

Use symptoms as a guide, not a test of harm. A small, short-lived increase can happen. Reduce the range, effort or repetitions if symptoms become sharp, keep building, or remain substantially worse afterwards.

Your physiotherapist will ask how your symptoms began, how they have changed, whether you have arm or hand symptoms, and which activities matter to you. Assessment may include neck and upper-back movement, arm strength and sensation, reflexes when indicated, and movements that reproduce or ease symptoms. This helps determine whether physiotherapy is suitable or whether medical review is needed.

Care is individualised. It may include a clear explanation, activity and work adjustments, progressive movement and strengthening, and strategies to return to driving, work, exercise or sport. Hands-on treatment may be used as a short-term adjunct when it suits your presentation, alongside an active plan. Progress and the plan are reviewed together.

Call 000 for sudden neck pain with chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, facial droop, difficulty speaking, sudden loss of balance or sudden weakness on one side. Seek urgent medical assessment after significant trauma, or for new or rapidly worsening arm or leg weakness, difficulty walking, loss of coordination, or new loss of bladder or bowel control.

Arrange prompt medical review for neck pain with fever or feeling very unwell, unexplained weight loss, a history of cancer, severe unrelenting pain, or new numbness or weakness. These features do not automatically mean a serious condition is present, but they need appropriate assessment. Arrange a GP or physiotherapy appointment if pain is not improving over a few weeks, is limiting sleep or daily activities, follows an injury, or you are unsure what to do.

Should I rest my neck?

Briefly easing off a task that strongly aggravates symptoms can help, but prolonged complete rest is usually not needed. Keep up comfortable movement and build activity back gradually.

Do I need an X-ray or MRI?

Not always. Imaging is considered when the history or examination suggests it is needed, or when the result is likely to change management. Your GP or physiotherapist can discuss the options with you.

What if pain travels into my arm?

Neck-related nerve irritation can sometimes cause pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in an arm or hand. These symptoms deserve assessment, especially if they are new, worsening or affecting strength or coordination.

How long will it take to improve?

Recovery varies depending on the cause, how long symptoms have been present, other health factors and the activities you want to return to. Many episodes improve over days to weeks, while some take longer. Seek review if symptoms worsen or are not changing as expected.

Persistent pain can make everyday movement feel difficult or uncertain. You may worry that activity will worsen symptoms, or find that doing a lot on a better day is followed by a flare. A flexible plan can help you stay connected to daily life and gradually build activity around your goals. You do not have to wait for pain to disappear before taking a manageable next step.

What is persistent pain?

Persistent pain is pain that continues or returns for longer than expected, often for three months or more. It can follow an injury or illness, occur with an ongoing health condition, or persist after tissues have healed. It is real and deserves care. Pain intensity does not always show how much tissue damage is present, although some conditions still need specific treatment or monitoring. A clinician can help consider the whole picture and check whether a new or changing symptom needs investigation.

Why staying active can help

Regular movement supports strength, mobility, cardiovascular health, sleep, mood and confidence. It can also help reduce the loss of capacity that may happen when pain leads to prolonged inactivity. The best starting point is an activity that feels relevant and achievable for you. It might be walking, gentle cycling, water-based activity, household tasks, gardening, mobility exercises or returning to a valued hobby.

Activity is one part of pain care. Your plan may also need to address sleep, stress, medication, work or caring demands, other health conditions and the things you want to get back to. If you have a condition that needs specific activity advice, follow your treating team’s recommendations.

Start with a manageable amount

Choose something that matters.
Pick one everyday activity or form of movement you would like to maintain or build.

Find a repeatable starting point.
Notice what you can do on an average day, not only on your best day. If activity levels vary, try a smaller amount that you can repeat more consistently.

Break it into shorter parts.
A few brief walks or movement breaks may be more manageable than one long session. You can also alternate heavier and lighter tasks.

Plan when to stop.
Set a time, distance or number of repetitions that is realistic. A planned pause can help prevent an unhelpful cycle of overdoing activity and then needing prolonged recovery.

Build gradually and flexibly.
When the current amount feels manageable and recovery is acceptable, increase one part at a time—such as duration, frequency or resistance. There is no single percentage or timetable that suits everyone.

Use symptoms as information, not a pass-or-fail test

Some people notice a temporary increase in pain when they change or increase activity. A small, short-lived increase does not automatically mean that harm has occurred. Pay attention to the pattern: what you did, how symptoms changed during the activity, and how you felt later that day and the next day. If symptoms keep rising, the activity becomes difficult to do safely, or you remain substantially worse afterwards, reduce the duration, range, speed or resistance and try a smaller step next time.

Try not to use pain alone to decide whether an activity is safe. Equally, you do not need to push through severe or escalating pain. Your diagnosis, health history, functional changes and recovery all matter. Ask your GP or physiotherapist for tailored advice if you are uncertain.

What to do during a flare-up

A flare-up is a period when pain or other symptoms temporarily become more intense. It can happen after a change in activity, poor sleep, illness, stress or for no obvious reason. A flare can be unsettling, but it does not always mean that your condition has deteriorated or that you have caused new damage.

Pause and reassess.
Look for a clear change from your usual symptoms or a new problem that needs medical review.

Adjust rather than abandon everything.
For a short time, use an easier version of the activity, shorten it, change position or spread it across the day. Keep comfortable movement and essential routines where you can.

Return in steps.
As the flare settles, resume from a manageable amount and build back towards your usual activity. Avoid trying to make up for missed activity all at once.

Review the plan.
If flares are frequent, prolonged or increasingly limiting, a clinician can help look at activity patterns and other contributors, and adjust the plan with you.

Make activity work in real life

Choose accessible options.
Use supportive footwear, a stable route, a chair or handrail, or water-based exercise if these make movement more comfortable or safe.

Add movement to routines.
Stand and move between tasks, take a short walk after a meal, or do a few gentle movements while waiting for the kettle. Small amounts count.

Protect recovery.
Plan activity around sleep, work, caring responsibilities and treatment. Regular meals, hydration and rest breaks can support participation.

Track function as well as pain.
Notice whether you are walking further, completing a task more comfortably, sleeping better or feeling more confident. Progress may show up in these ways even when pain remains.

When to get advice

Seek urgent medical care for new or rapidly worsening symptoms, such as progressive limb weakness, new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle area, or severe pain after a significant injury. Call 000 for symptoms that may be an emergency, including chest pressure with severe breathlessness, fainting or feeling acutely unwell.

A note about delayed symptom worsening

If physical or mental activity causes a delayed and prolonged worsening of symptoms—sometimes called post-exertional symptom exacerbation—do not follow a standard graded exercise plan without clinical guidance. This can occur in conditions such as ME/CFS and some post-viral illnesses. Seek advice from a clinician familiar with your condition so your activity plan accounts for your response.

Sport can be a rewarding part of adolescence, offering opportunities to develop skills, build friendships and enjoy being active. As young athletes grow, their training needs can change. Supporting progress means considering the whole person, including growth, school demands, recovery and enjoyment—not just results.

This guide explains how families can support young athletes through those changes and when physiotherapy or medical assessment may be helpful.

Growth changes the training picture

Young people of the same age can be at very different stages of physical development. A change in height, strength or coordination may affect how training feels, and comparisons with teammates do not always reflect an athlete’s progress. Growth and maturation are associated with changing injury patterns, although individual responses vary. [1, 2]

Pain around the knee or heel can occur during the growing years, including conditions such as Osgood–Schlatter disease and Sever’s disease. Persistent pain should not simply be dismissed as growing pains. An assessment can help identify the cause and guide appropriate activity.

Look at the whole training week

Club sessions are only part of an athlete’s workload. School sport, gym sessions, competitions and additional practice all contribute. Exams, travel and early starts can also reduce opportunities to recover. [3]

Keep a shared weekly calendar and speak with coaches when demands overlap. Build training gradually, particularly after illness, injury or a break. There is no single weekly schedule that suits every adolescent athlete.

For example, a swimmer’s week might include pool training, dryland sessions and a school carnival. Adding extra gym work during that week should involve discussion about the total demands and available recovery, rather than treating each activity separately.

Build strength with appropriate guidance

Resistance training can improve strength and aspects of physical performance in young athletes. Programmes should match the athlete’s experience, development and goals, with appropriate supervision and gradual progression. [1, 4]

A programme might include squatting, stepping, pushing, pulling and landing tasks. The choice of exercises and resistance should reflect the individual and their sport. Consistent training and learning movement skills matter more than chasing heavier weights.

Make recovery part of the plan

Training provides a stimulus; recovery allows adaptation. Regular meals and snacks, access to fluids, sufficient sleep and planned time away from demanding training help support an athlete’s health. Ongoing fatigue deserves attention rather than automatically being treated as a sign to train harder. [1, 3]

Teenagers aged 13–18 are generally recommended to sleep 8–10 hours per 24 hours. Early training sessions need to be considered alongside bedtime and school commitments. [5]

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends aiming for at least 1–2 days each week away from competition and sport-specific training. Discuss how to plan recovery with coaches, particularly when an athlete trains or competes across several teams. [3]

If eating enough is difficult, fatigue persists or there are concerns about growth or menstrual health, seek advice from a GP and an accredited sports dietitian.

Know when to seek assessment

Arrange assessment when pain keeps returning, changes how an athlete moves or limits participation. Other reasons to seek advice include swelling, limping, difficulty with daily activities or a sustained decline in performance accompanied by fatigue.

Seek prompt medical assessment for inability to bear weight, significant swelling or deformity after injury, pain associated with fever or feeling unwell, or unexplained pain at rest or at night.

Pain does not always mean that all activity must stop, but the cause matters. A treating professional can help decide what to modify, what can continue and how to progress safely.

Physiotherapy may help clarify a pain or movement concern and develop a plan suited to the athlete’s needs. Depending on the assessment, this can include exercise, changes to training, rehabilitation and a gradual return to sport. Hands-on treatment may be used where appropriate alongside active rehabilitation.

Strength and movement testing can help guide goals and monitor progress. These assessments cannot reliably predict whether an individual athlete will be injured. Decisions should also consider symptoms, sporting demands and recovery.

[SOURCE: https://strideshealth.com.au/resources/growth-related-sports-pain]
Pain around the knee, heel or hip can appear during a young athlete’s growth years. Growth can change how the body responds to training, but pain should not automatically be dismissed as “growing pains.” Repetitive sport loads, a recent training increase, recovery, strength and other health factors may all matter. A clear assessment helps identify the likely cause and lets the athlete stay as active as is safe.

What does growth-related sports pain mean?

Some young athletes develop irritation where a tendon or muscle attaches to a growth area of bone. These attachment growth areas are called apophyses. Repeated running, jumping, kicking, throwing or other forceful actions can irritate them, particularly while the skeleton is still maturing. These problems are often called apophysitis. They are different from ordinary muscle soreness and from growth in height itself.

Growth is one part of the picture, not a diagnosis. A young person can have sport-related pain for many reasons, and similar symptoms may need a different response. Growth-related pain is more often gradual and localised, and tends to increase with the activity that loads the area. A sudden injury, pain at rest or other concerning symptoms should be assessed.

Common examples in young athletes

Osgood–Schlatter: pain and tenderness at the bony bump just below the kneecap, often worse with running, jumping, squatting or kneeling [1, 2].

Sever’s disease (calcaneal apophysitis): pain at the back or underside of the heel, commonly during running and jumping [1, 5, 6].

Sinding–Larsen–Johansson: pain at the lower edge of the kneecap, often related to jumping or running.

Pelvic apophysitis: pain near the hip or pelvis where powerful muscles attach; a sudden forceful kick or sprint can occasionally cause an avulsion injury.

These are examples, not a checklist for self-diagnosis. Pain location, the way it began, the athlete’s age and development, training history and examination findings all help guide the diagnosis.

Why can symptoms show up during a busy season?

A change in growth and maturation can coincide with changes in coordination, muscle–tendon flexibility and how training feels. At the same time, athletes may add team sessions, school sport, competitions, gym work or extra practice. A rapid change in the type, frequency or intensity of loading can leave less time to adapt. This does not mean sport is harmful during growth; it means symptoms and the whole training week deserve attention.

Tell someone early. The athlete should tell a parent or guardian and coach. Note where it hurts, what activity brings it on and whether it is changing movement or daily activities.

Adjust the painful load. Temporarily reduce or modify the actions that clearly aggravate symptoms, such as repeated sprints, jumps, hills, kicking or deep knee loading. Do not push through pain that causes a limp, changes technique or worsens as the session continues.

Keep suitable activity where possible. Depending on the site and symptoms, pain-free or less-irritating training may be possible. A physiotherapist or doctor can help decide what to modify and what can continue. Complete rest is not automatically required, and the best option varies by athlete and condition.

Build back progressively. When everyday movement and sport-specific tasks are settling, increase the amount and intensity in stages. Strength and conditioning may help restore capacity, but exercises should match the athlete’s symptoms, sport and development.

For some adolescents with Osgood–Schlatter, a prospective cohort study found improved function after education, temporary activity modification and progressive strengthening [3]. Evidence for the best treatment is still limited, particularly across different conditions [2, 5]. For Sever’s disease, a trial comparing heel raises and orthoses found that several options could help, with no single approach clearly best for everyone [4]. A tailored plan and symptom response matter.

When should an athlete be assessed?

Arrange an assessment if pain is recurring, persists, is getting worse, causes a limp, limits training or everyday activities, or is accompanied by swelling or a marked loss of movement. Assessment is also useful when the cause is unclear or the athlete is trying to plan around competitions. A physiotherapist or doctor can examine the area, consider other causes and advise whether imaging or referral is needed.

Please seek prompt medical assessment.
Sudden severe pain after a forceful movement or fall, inability to bear weight, a large or rapidly developing swelling, a hot/red joint, fever or feeling unwell, or pain that is persistent at rest or wakes the athlete at night.

A sports physiotherapy assessment considers the athlete’s symptoms, growth and development, sport demands, recent training changes, recovery and goals. We can help clarify what activities to modify, maintain useful training where appropriate, develop a progressive rehabilitation plan and coordinate with parents, coaches or the treating doctor when suitable.

Swimming repeats the arm-overhead movement many times, especially during training. Shoulder pain is reported by competitive swimmers, but it is not just a young-swimmer issue or something to hide and push through. A calm plan to understand symptoms and adjust training can help a swimmer stay as active as possible.

What people mean by swimmer’s shoulder

“Swimmer’s shoulder” is an informal umbrella term for shoulder pain linked with swimming. It is not one specific diagnosis. Pain can arise from several structures and may be influenced by the amount and type of swimming, recent changes in training, recovery, previous shoulder problems, strength and swimming mechanics. A physiotherapist or doctor can assess what is contributing for an individual swimmer.

Shoulder pain across ages and levels

Studies report shoulder symptoms in swimmers from youth through adult and masters competition, but estimates vary because studies look at different age groups, training levels and time periods. A U.S. survey of 671 competitive swimmers aged 9–17 found that 49% reported symptoms; this describes that sample and does not estimate pain in recreational or all-age swimmers. [1,2]

Why shoulders can become sore

Swimming involves repeated arm movements. Shoulder symptoms can reflect several factors, including recent changes in training volume or intensity, previous pain, recovery and strength. A systematic review found swim-training volume was associated with shoulder pain in adolescent competitive swimmers, but it did not establish a universal safe distance or a single cause that applies to every swimmer. [2,3] Consider changes in paddles, sessions, competition, gym or other upper-body work alongside sleep, recovery and other life demands. The right training adjustment depends on the individual.

Growth and development in younger swimmers

For children and teenagers, growth and development can change body size and how training feels. Growth itself is not an injury, and technique is not automatically the cause. Adjust training and strength work to the swimmer in front of you.

Tell someone you trust and, if you train with a coach, let them know early. A young swimmer should also tell a parent or trusted adult. Pain that changes a stroke, worsens during a session, returns repeatedly, or lingers after training deserves attention.

Avoid repeatedly testing the painful stroke or trying to “swim it off”. Temporarily reduce or modify the parts of training that provoke symptoms while keeping comfortable activity where appropriate.

Make a short note of when it hurts: which stroke, drill, distance or intensity; when it starts; what makes it better or worse; and how it feels later that day and the next morning.

Review recent training and other demands: changes in pool volume, intensity, equipment, gym work, other sports, work or school, sleep and recovery. There is no single workload formula that can diagnose or prevent an individual swimmer’s shoulder pain.

Arrange an assessment if symptoms persist, recur, limit training or everyday tasks, affect stroke technique, or are getting worse. A physiotherapist or doctor can assess the shoulder and help plan training modifications and rehabilitation.

Building shoulder capacity

Progressive strength training can be part of preparation for swimmers of different ages and levels. Programs often target the shoulder external rotators and scapular muscles; mobility work may be included when it is relevant. A scoping review found exercise therapy has been studied in competitive swimmers, but the evidence does not support one universal program or guarantee that injury will be prevented. [4]

A physiotherapist, coach or appropriately qualified strength and conditioning professional can help choose exercises that fit the swimmer’s age, training experience and needs, teach technique and progress resistance gradually. The goal is comfortable, controlled work that fits around swimming, not extra fatigue layered onto an already demanding week.

Returning to full swimming

Return should be guided by symptoms, shoulder function and the swimmer’s sport demands. In general, the swimmer should be able to move the arm comfortably, manage daily activities, and complete a gradual progression of swimming without pain escalating or lingering afterwards. Increase one training demand at a time where possible, and coordinate the plan with a coach and treating clinician if involved. A race date alone is not a reason to rush.

Call 000 if shoulder pain occurs with chest tightness or difficulty breathing. After a shoulder injury, seek immediate medical care if the joint looks deformed, the arm cannot be used, the pain is intense or there is sudden swelling. Arrange medical assessment for persistent or worsening pain, pain at rest, fever, night sweats, unexplained weight loss, or new weakness or numbness. [5]

A sports physiotherapy assessment can consider symptoms, shoulder and upper-limb capacity, training history, age and development, recovery, stroke demands and goals. We can work with the swimmer and, where appropriate, their family and coach to agree on practical training modifications and a progressive rehabilitation plan.

Moving around home, getting out of a chair or taking part in a community activity can become more difficult when a health condition affects movement. Physiotherapy may help you work towards everyday goals, maintain abilities or adapt activities as your needs change.

This resource explains the role of physiotherapy, what an assessment may involve and how to prepare. The starting point is what matters to you, including your choices about assistance, equipment and participation.

Different people need different support

Neurological conditions affect the brain, spinal cord or nerves and can influence strength, balance, sensation, coordination or fatigue. Disability can involve a wide range of physical, developmental and other needs. Two people with the same diagnosis may have very different abilities and priorities.

A plan therefore considers your health, current function, environment and preferences. Depending on your condition, the focus may be learning or recovering skills, maintaining function, managing symptoms or finding a safer or more manageable way to do a task. Rehabilitation goals should be individualised. [1]

Start with an everyday goal

A meaningful goal might be transferring from a chair with less assistance, walking to the letterbox, participating in a class or feeling more comfortable during daily routines. Using appropriate assistance or equipment can support independence and choice.

The 2025 NICE guideline on rehabilitation for chronic neurological disorders recommends agreeing goals collaboratively and breaking longer-term goals into smaller steps. Goals and review timing should reflect the person’s condition and situation. [2]

For example, a goal of getting to a local café might involve transfers, walking or wheelchair access, endurance and transport. The plan may require more than one professional, and progress can be measured against participation as well as physical tests.

We begin with your concerns, relevant medical history, current support and the activities you want to manage more easily. You can tell us what helps, what makes tasks harder and how symptoms vary through the day.

Depending on your needs, assessment may consider:

Strength, joint movement, sensation, coordination and balance.

Walking, wheelchair mobility or moving between positions.

Transfers, such as getting into or out of a chair or bed.

Pain, fatigue, falls and how these affect your daily activities.

Your current mobility aids, equipment and level of assistance.

The assessment is adapted to your abilities and communication needs. Tell us about helpful instructions, sensory preferences, rest breaks or support you would like during the appointment. A family member, carer or support worker can attend if you choose.

How physiotherapy may contribute

Depending on the assessment and your condition, care may include practice of meaningful tasks, tailored exercise, balance or walking activities, positioning advice and strategies for managing activity. The 2025 NICE guideline recommends developing exercise and activity programmes around physical function and individual barriers, including fatigue or pain. [3]

Some people need advice about mobility aids or equipment. Physiotherapy can contribute to assessment and practical use, with referral or collaboration where specialist equipment, wheelchair seating or home modifications are required.

There is no single exercise programme suitable for all neurological conditions or disabilities. The expected benefits, appropriate intensity and precautions depend on the diagnosis and the person. Any exercise plan should account for relevant medical advice and be reviewed over time.

Working with fatigue and changing needs

Fatigue may affect when and how activities are manageable. Tell your treating professional whether it follows physical effort, varies with sleep or medication, or has changed recently. A plan might adjust task length, timing, breaks or assistance rather than simply increasing exercise.

New or unexplained fatigue needs consideration by your GP or treating team. Changes in function should prompt review of the plan and, where appropriate, medical assessment. Rehabilitation may focus on maintaining participation or adapting support as well as improving physical abilities. [3]

Working with your wider team

Care may involve your GP, specialist, occupational therapist, speech pathologist, psychologist, orthotist or other professionals. With your consent, we can share relevant findings and coordinate goals. Family members and support workers can also help carry agreed strategies into everyday routines. [1, 4]

If another person assists with movement or transfers, the technique and equipment should be assessed for the individual situation. Ask for practical guidance before trying a new transfer method or changing the amount of assistance.

Preparing for your appointment

Bring relevant medical or therapy reports, a current medication list and the mobility aids you usually use, where practical. Think about one or two activities that matter most to you and any recent changes, falls or difficulties.

Contact the clinic beforehand about access, transfer assistance, communication support or other adjustments. This helps us plan the appointment and confirm whether the setting and available support suit your needs.

When medical care takes priority

Sudden new neurological symptoms should not wait for a routine physiotherapy appointment. Call 000 immediately for possible stroke symptoms such as new facial drooping, arm weakness or difficulty speaking, even if they resolve. [5]

Seek prompt medical advice for an unexplained deterioration in strength, walking or function, repeated new falls or a significant change from your usual symptoms. If symptoms are sudden or severe, seek emergency care.

Movement, function and participation

Multiple sclerosis (MS) affects the central nervous system and can influence movement, balance, strength, sensation and energy in different ways. Physiotherapy can help you work towards meaningful everyday goals with a plan adapted to your symptoms, abilities and priorities. It does not replace care from your neurologist or change the underlying disease process.

What is multiple sclerosis?

MS is a long-term condition involving inflammation and damage in the brain and spinal cord. The course and effects vary considerably: symptoms may change over time, fluctuate from day to day, or affect different people in different ways. A person’s physiotherapy needs can therefore change over time too.

How MS can affect movement and daily life

MS may affect walking, transfers, balance, coordination, strength, muscle tone and endurance. Some people experience stiffness or spasms, altered sensation, pain, visual changes, bladder or bowel symptoms, or fatigue. Heat, infection, poor sleep and other health factors can also affect how a person feels and moves. Not everyone experiences all of these changes.

A physiotherapist can assess how MS is affecting the activities that matter to you and develop a practical plan with you. Depending on your goals, this may include:

Strength and aerobic exercise to support fitness, strength and physical capacity.

Balance, coordination and walking practice, including turning, stairs and negotiating uneven surfaces.

Transfers and everyday movement such as getting up from a chair, moving around the home or accessing the community.

Strategies for stiffness, muscle tightness, positioning and maintaining comfortable movement.

Fatigue planning, pacing and ways to adapt activity across better and more difficult days.

Falls risk review, walking-aid advice and recommendations for equipment or home set-up where appropriate.

A home or community programme that is reviewed as your symptoms, health and goals change.

Exercise with MS

Regular physical activity is encouraged for people with MS. Exercise does not appear to increase the risk of an MS relapse, and it can support fitness, strength, mobility and general health. NICE recommends considering supervised programmes that combine moderate, progressive resistance and aerobic exercise for people with MS who have mobility problems or fatigue [1].

International exercise recommendations describe 150 minutes per week of exercise and/or lifestyle physical activity as a potential longer-term target, while emphasising individual assessment and tailoring for symptoms, ability and other health conditions [2]. This is not a starting requirement or a pass/fail goal. A smaller, manageable amount is a useful beginning, and activity can be built up gradually with advice from your healthcare team.

Making activity manageable

Choose activities that connect with your goals and can be adjusted on the day.

Use shorter sessions or planned rests if fatigue makes longer sessions difficult.

Plan demanding activities for the time of day when you usually have more energy.

Stay cool where possible. A rise in body temperature can temporarily bring on or worsen familiar MS symptoms for some people; this usually settles as you cool down and is not necessarily a relapse.

Pause or modify the session if you feel unwell, unusually weak, dizzy, unsafe or significantly more fatigued. Ask your clinician how to adjust your plan.

Fatigue and pacing

MS fatigue can have several contributors, including the condition itself, sleep disruption, pain, mood, medications, infection and reduced conditioning. A physiotherapist can help you identify activity patterns, balance activity with recovery and build exercise progressively. Persistent or changing fatigue is worth discussing with your GP or MS team, particularly if it is new or affecting daily life.

Contact your neurologist, MS nurse or GP about new, significant or persistent symptoms, a marked change in walking or balance, repeated falls, or symptoms that are not settling as expected. Do not assume a new symptom is simply an MS fluctuation; your treating team can help assess possible causes. Call 000 for sudden severe symptoms or an emergency such as facial droop, difficulty speaking, severe weakness, chest pain or serious breathing difficulty.

Physiotherapy is one part of MS care. With your consent, your physiotherapist can work alongside your neurologist, GP, occupational therapist and other members of your team. Bring your questions, current goals and any relevant walking aid or equipment to an appointment. Let your physiotherapist know what has changed and what helps you feel safe.

A practical guide to movement, exercise and support

Parkinson’s disease can affect movement in different ways and at different times. Physiotherapy can help you work towards the activities that matter to you, with an individual plan for mobility, balance, strength, fitness and confidence. The right approach depends on your symptoms, health, goals and safety needs.

What is Parkinson’s disease?

Parkinson’s disease is a progressive neurological condition associated with changes in the brain that affect movement. Symptoms and their impact vary from person to person. Medication and support from a multidisciplinary team can help manage symptoms; physiotherapy is one part of this care.

Movement changes can include slowness, stiffness, tremor, smaller movements, reduced arm swing, changes in posture, balance difficulties, shorter steps, or freezing (a brief feeling that the feet are stuck, often when starting, turning or passing through a narrow space). Not everyone experiences all of these.

Parkinson’s can also affect sleep, energy, mood, thinking, speech, swallowing, pain, blood pressure, bladder or bowel function. Tell your GP or specialist about changes, even when they do not seem directly related to movement.

A physiotherapist can assess how Parkinson’s affects your daily activities and develop a plan with you. This may include:

• practising walking, turning, getting up from a chair, bed mobility and stairs;• balance, strength, flexibility, aerobic fitness and posture exercises;• movement strategies and external cues, such as a visual target, rhythm or a clear verbal prompt, for small steps or freezing;• advice on pacing, falls risk, walking aids, footwear, home setup and safe ways to stay active; and• a home or community exercise plan that fits your routine and changes as your needs change.

Exercise and staying active

Regular physical activity is encouraged for people living with Parkinson’s. Exercise can support mobility, fitness, strength, balance, function and quality of life. A physiotherapist can help choose activities and progress them safely, especially if you have fallen, freeze, feel dizzy, have osteoporosis, or have other health conditions.

A balanced program may include aerobic activity, strength training, balance and agility practice, movement with larger and more purposeful actions, and flexibility work. The amount and intensity should be individualised. If you are inactive or have complex health or safety concerns, seek advice before starting vigorous or challenging exercise.

Choose activities you enjoy and can maintain. Walking, cycling, dancing, tai chi, swimming or a supervised exercise class may suit some people. If balance is reduced, use an appropriate support or supervision and avoid practising challenging balance tasks alone.

Practical strategies for freezing and small steps

If your feet feel stuck, pause and reset rather than rushing. Try one strategy at a time, such as shifting your weight, looking at a clear target, stepping over an imagined line, counting aloud, or following a steady rhythm. A physiotherapist can help identify which cues work for you and practise them in the places where freezing occurs.

When turning, try making a wider curve with several deliberate steps instead of pivoting quickly. Keep pathways clear, allow time at doorways and when changing direction, and avoid carrying objects that block your view or hands if this affects balance. These strategies are not a substitute for an individual assessment.

What happens at an appointment?

Your physiotherapist will ask what you want to keep doing or find easier, review your movement and falls history, and assess relevant activities such as walking, balance, transfers, strength and mobility. You may be asked about medication timing and how your movement changes through the day. Wear comfortable clothing and bring your usual walking aid, footwear and a current medication list if available.

Together, you can set practical goals and agree on treatment, a home plan and review timing. Your plan may be coordinated with your GP, neurologist, occupational therapist, speech pathologist or other members of your care team, with your consent.

When to seek help

Contact your GP, neurologist or treating team if you notice a new or worsening change in walking, balance, falls, dizziness, pain, swallowing, memory, mood or ability to manage daily activities. An unexpected change may have causes other than Parkinson’s and should be assessed.

If someone has a serious injury, cannot be woken, has sudden facial droop or weakness, sudden difficulty speaking, chest pain, or severe breathing difficulty, call 000 in Australia.

If you would like to discuss whether physiotherapy may suit your goals, contact the clinic or book an appointment. Supporting movement, function and participation after a stroke

The focus of rehabilitation is what matters to you: practising movement and everyday tasks in a way that is individualised, progressive and safe.

What happens in a stroke?

A stroke occurs when the blood supply to part of the brain is interrupted, usually by a blocked blood vessel or bleeding. The affected area can no longer work as it usually does. Because different parts of the brain control movement, sensation, vision, communication and thinking, the effects depend on where and how much the brain is affected.

Common effects can include weakness or reduced control on one side, difficulty coordinating movement, changes in sensation or muscle stiffness, and problems with sitting balance, standing or walking. Fatigue, pain, reduced confidence and changes in vision, communication, memory or mood can also affect daily activities. Some changes are less visible but still have a major impact. Every person’s combination of effects is different.

Recovery is individual

A stroke can affect movement, balance, coordination, sensation, strength, endurance and the ability to manage everyday activities. The effects differ from person to person, and they can change over time. Rehabilitation is guided by the person’s goals, abilities, health and preferences.

Physiotherapy may begin in hospital when the treating team considers it safe, then continue in inpatient rehabilitation, at home, in an outpatient clinic or through a combination of services. A plan can also be reviewed later if needs or goals change.

A physiotherapist assesses how the stroke is affecting movement and daily life, then works with the person to set practical goals. Depending on individual needs, physiotherapy may focus on:

An assessment looks at the activities you want or need to do and what may be making them difficult. Depending on your needs, this can include movement and strength on both sides, balance in sitting and standing, transfers, walking, coordination, sensation, muscle stiffness, pain, fatigue and activity tolerance. Your physiotherapist may also ask about falls, daily routines, mobility aids, home access and the support available to you.

This information helps shape a plan around your priorities and current abilities. Progress is reviewed over time, and goals or supports can be adjusted as your needs change. A physiotherapy assessment is not just a test of strength; it considers how movement works in real activities and environments.

Walking, transfers and bed mobility; balance and confidence with everyday movement; strength, coordination and control of the affected arm or leg; fitness and activity tolerance; posture, flexibility and comfort; and practising tasks that matter to the person, such as standing from a chair, managing steps or walking outdoors.

Rehabilitation is not limited to the first weeks or months. People may continue to make gains or benefit from support at different stages after stroke. Progress is individual and cannot be predicted from one person’s experience.

What sessions may involve

Sessions are guided by assessment and shared goals. They may include repeated practice of meaningful tasks, walking and balance activities, progressive strengthening, aerobic exercise when appropriate, and strategies to make movement safer and easier in daily routines. Practice might include repeated sit-to-stands, a transfer, stepping or walking a route that matters in your day.

The physiotherapist adjusts the challenge, support, equipment and rest to your capacity. Fatigue, falls risk, pain, other health conditions, communication or cognitive changes, and your home environment can affect how practice is planned. The plan can be reviewed if your function or health changes.

Where it is suitable, practice between appointments can provide extra opportunities to work towards goals. A home program should be clear, manageable and safe, with an agreed plan for how much to practise and when to pause or seek advice.

Stroke rehabilitation is often multidisciplinary. Physiotherapists may coordinate with the person’s GP, rehabilitation physician, occupational therapist, speech pathologist, nurse, psychologist and other members of the care team. Occupational therapists can help with daily activities and equipment; speech pathologists can support communication and swallowing; and the medical team can advise on health issues that affect exercise and recovery.

Family members and support people can be involved with the person’s consent. Useful support can include helping communicate goals, making the home practice safer, and sharing observations about what is changing.

Call 000 immediately if new or sudden symptoms suggest another stroke, even if they improve. Use FAST: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, Time to call 000. Do not wait for a physiotherapy appointment.

Contact the treating doctor or physiotherapist if there is a new fall, increasing pain, a noticeable change in movement, worsening fatigue, or a concern that current exercises or mobility supports are no longer safe.
